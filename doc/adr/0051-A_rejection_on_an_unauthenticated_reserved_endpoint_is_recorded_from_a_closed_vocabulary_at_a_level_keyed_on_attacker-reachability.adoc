== Status

Proposed

== Context

A gateway mounts reserved endpoints that are, by protocol design, reachable *without a credential*.
A protocol peer posts to them unauthenticated and the endpoint decides, from the request's own
content, whether to act. The OIDC back-channel logout receiver is the archetype: any host that can
reach the gateway can post to it, and the only thing separating a genuine identity-provider delivery
from noise is the signature on the token in the body.

Such an endpoint rejects far more often than it acts, and each rejection poses the same two questions
— *what may be said about it*, and *how loudly*. Both were being answered by reflex rather than by
decision, and each reflex has its own failure mode.

*Silence is indistinguishable from absence.* Recording every rejection at `DEBUG` makes the whole
path invisible at the default log level. A delivery that never arrived and a delivery that arrived
and was refused produce byte-identical silence. The operator question behind an unexpected logout
behaviour — *did the identity provider even reach us?* — then has no answer short of raising the
level and waiting for the condition to recur, which on a cross-service integration may never happen
on demand.

*Volume is a denial-of-service surface.* Recording every rejection at `WARN` inverts the problem.
The path is unauthenticated, so an unbounded `WARN` flood is available to anyone who can reach the
gateway — no session, no credential, not even a well-formed body. A log sink that an anonymous caller
can drive is a resource, and the cost lands on the operator's storage, their alerting, and their
ability to see anything else.

*A reason string is a sink.* Whatever level is chosen, the rejection wants to say *why*. The
tempting shape is to interpolate the offending detail: the claim that failed, the value that was
wrong, the subject it concerned. On this path that detail is attacker-supplied and may be
identity-bearing — a `sub`, a `sid`, a fragment of a token. A free-text reason parameter is a
general-purpose exfiltration channel into the log, and once the parameter is free-text, nothing
structural stops the next caller from filling it.

The two questions are not independent, and that is the structural observation. *Which* rejections an
anonymous caller can drive is not a property of the endpoint — it is a property of each individual
reason, and it is decided by exactly one thing: whether that reason sits before or after the
signature check. A reason reachable only after verification cannot be reached by an attacker at all,
because an attacker cannot mint a token the configured issuer signed.

== Decision

*A rejection on a reserved endpoint reachable without a credential is recorded through one emission
point, from a closed enumerated vocabulary, at a level keyed on whether an unauthenticated caller can
reach that reason.*

Three coupled mechanisms realise it.

=== (1) The reason vocabulary is closed and enumerated, and carries no value

Every rejection reason is a member of a fixed enumeration, and each member's only payload is a fixed
lower-case token. No token material, no `sub`, no `sid`, no raw offending value. The structured log
record's single placeholder accepts a member of that enumeration and nothing else, so the record
*cannot widen into a free-text sink* — the constraint is carried by the type, not by a review
convention that the next caller has to be told about.

The closure is the load-bearing half. A bounded-but-open vocabulary — a string the caller is asked to
keep short and non-sensitive — has exactly the failure mode the closed one removes: it works until
someone needs one more detail, and the parameter admits it.

=== (2) The level discriminator is attacker-reachability, carried by the vocabulary itself

Each member declares whether it is reachable only *after* the credential has been cryptographically
verified. That one fact is the level discriminator, and it lives on the member rather than at the
call sites, so adding a reason forces the question to be answered once, at the point the reason is
defined.

* *Post-verification reasons* are reachable only by a token the configured identity provider actually
  signed. An attacker cannot mint one, so there is no flood to bound: they are recorded at `WARN` on
  every occurrence. These are the reasons that carry real operator signal — a misconfigured audience,
  a clock outside the replay window, a token missing the protocol's own required claims.
* *Pre-verification reasons* are attacker-triggerable, so they are *latched*: the first occurrence of
  each reason in a process is recorded at `WARN`, and every repeat drops to `DEBUG`.

=== (3) The flood bound is a per-reason, per-process latch

The latch bounds the flood to at most one line per reason for the lifetime of the process, while the
first real occurrence of each reason still reaches the default log level — which is the point. The
operator learns *that* the condition occurred without the volume being attacker-controlled.

The bound applies per assembled runtime: each component that can reject holds its own emitter, bound
to its own logger, so the record is attributed to the class that actually refused the request rather
than to a shared logging utility.

=== What the latch deliberately does not give

Absence of a repeated `WARN` says *nothing about the rate*. An attacker who consumes a reason's latch
early makes a later genuine occurrence of that same reason `DEBUG`-only. Both are accepted costs,
named here so a future reader does not rediscover them as defects: the `WARN` channel answers *did
this happen at all*, and the `DEBUG` channel answers *how often* and *is it still happening*.

== Consequences

=== Positive

* The unauthenticated path is no longer invisible. An operator at the default log level can tell a
  delivery that never arrived from one that arrived and was refused — and which refusal it was.
* The log-volume surface an anonymous caller can drive is bounded by construction, at one line per
  reason per process, rather than by the caller's restraint.
* The rejection record cannot leak identity-bearing or attacker-supplied data, because there is no
  parameter that could carry it. Secure logging is a property of the type, not of each call site.
* The reasons that carry the most operator signal are the ones recorded unconditionally. Level
  follows value, and the correlation is not a coincidence: the reasons only a genuinely signed token
  reaches are exactly the ones describing a real integration problem.
* Adding a rejection reason forces its reachability to be classified at the point of definition. The
  question cannot be skipped, because the enumeration member does not compile without an answer.
* Every rejection flows through one emission point, so the flood policy is stated once and cannot be
  partially applied.

=== Negative

* The `WARN` channel understates rate for latched reasons, permanently and by design. An operator
  diagnosing a *volume* question must read `DEBUG`, and must know to.
* A latch consumed by an attacker demotes a later genuine occurrence of that same reason. The first
  occurrence is guaranteed; the *informative* one is not.
* The closed vocabulary is a maintenance obligation: a rejection whose reason is not in the set
  cannot be recorded with a reason at all until the set is extended, which is a deliberate change
  rather than a local one.
* The record is coarser than a free-text one. An operator learns the reason class, not the offending
  value, and closing the remaining gap is a `DEBUG`-level or upstream-side exercise.
* Latch state is per process and per assembled runtime, so a restart re-arms every latch and two
  runtimes in one process each emit their own first `WARN`. The bound is "one per reason per
  emitter", not a global one.

=== Risks

* *The latch being read as a rate limiter.* It is not: it is a one-shot per reason. A reader who
  expects sampled `WARN`s under sustained load will see exactly one and conclude the condition
  stopped.
* *Erosion of the closed vocabulary.* Each future request to add "just this one value" to the record
  is individually plausible and collectively reconstructs the free-text sink. The closure is the
  limit, not a starting point.
* *Mis-classification of a reason's reachability.* A reason wrongly marked post-verification
  reinstates the unbounded flood for that reason. The declaration sits on the member where it is
  reviewable, but nothing computes it from the code path.
* *The discriminator drifting from the pipeline.* If a check moves from after the signature stage to
  before it, its reason's classification must move with it, and no mechanism enforces that coupling.

== Alternatives Considered

*Record every rejection at `DEBUG`.* This is the minimal-risk choice on volume and leaks nothing by
level. Rejected because it makes an entire protocol integration unobservable at the level operators
actually run: the two states an operator needs to distinguish — nothing arrived, versus something
arrived and was refused — are rendered identically as silence, and the distinction can only be
recovered by reproducing a cross-service condition on demand.

*Record every rejection at `WARN`.* This maximises signal and needs no per-reason classification.
Rejected because it hands an unbounded, unauthenticated write into the operator's log sink to anyone
who can reach the gateway. A defence that an anonymous caller can turn into a resource-exhaustion
vector is not a defence, and "the path is rarely hit" is an assumption about the absence of an
adversary.

*Rate-limit the emission with a token bucket instead of a latch.* This preserves the rate signal
under load and still bounds the volume. Rejected as disproportionate for what it buys here: it
introduces per-reason time state and a refill policy — two knobs with no obviously correct values —
to improve a channel that already carries the rate question at `DEBUG`. The latch is one
concurrent-set insertion and states its own limitation plainly; the bucket would state a
rate-fidelity promise it only approximately keeps.

*Discriminate on request origin — trusted source address, mTLS peer — instead of on
attacker-reachability.* This is the intuitive reading of "can an attacker drive this". Rejected
because it makes the log policy depend on deployment-supplied network trust, which is exactly the
input a reserved unauthenticated endpoint must not rely on for a security property. The signature
check is the boundary the protocol itself defines; deriving the discriminator from it keeps the
policy correct under every network topology, including the one where the gateway is directly
reachable.

*Keep a free-text reason parameter with a documented "no sensitive values" rule.* This preserves
full diagnostic detail and costs nothing to implement. Rejected because it relocates a structural
guarantee into a convention. The parameter's type would admit a `sub`, a `sid`, or a token fragment,
and the only thing preventing it is that each future caller remembers. A sink that is safe by
discipline is a sink.

*Let each rejection site choose its own level.* This is the most flexible shape and needs no central
emitter. Rejected because the flood policy is exactly the kind of rule that must hold *everywhere*
to hold at all: one site choosing unconditional `WARN` for an attacker-reachable reason removes the
bound for the whole endpoint, and nothing would surface that it had.

The rejected alternatives divide cleanly. One group picks a single global level — all `DEBUG` or all
`WARN` — and so must sacrifice either observability or the volume bound, because it refuses to let
the two rejection classes differ. The other keeps the distinction but grounds it in something
weaker than the protocol's own trust boundary: network origin, caller discipline, or per-site
judgement. The chosen position derives the discriminator from the one fact that actually decides
attacker-reachability, and carries both it and the reason vocabulary in the type system so neither
can be partially applied.
